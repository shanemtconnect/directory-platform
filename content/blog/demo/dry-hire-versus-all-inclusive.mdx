Venues broadly sell you one of two things: a space you fill yourself, or a day someone else runs. Between them sits everything from "our caterer, your florist" to "our list of five approved suppliers".

Neither is cheaper in principle. Which one is cheaper *for you* depends on how much of the work you are able and willing to do.

## What each one actually means

### Dry hire

You rent the building and the hours. Catering, drink, furniture, staff, lighting, cleaning and often the licence for the ceremony are yours to arrange.

**It suits you if** you want an unusual space, you have strong opinions about food, you already know suppliers you trust, or you are happy to spend evenings coordinating them.

**Watch for:** the number of things that are not in the hire fee. Furniture, glassware, a kitchen that a caterer can actually work in, power for a band, waste removal, and someone to be there when the first van arrives at eight in the morning.

### All-inclusive

One contract, one contact, a package that covers most of the day. Choices are made from a menu rather than from the open market.

**It suits you if** you would rather make ten decisions than a hundred, you want a predictable total early, or you are planning at a distance from where the event will be.

**Watch for:** what "inclusive" excludes. Packages commonly stop at photography, flowers, music, cake and transport, and the per-head price often assumes a particular drinks selection.

### The middle ground

Most venues sit here: their kitchen, your florist. This is often the sensible answer, but it is also where quotes become hardest to compare, because each venue draws the line in a different place. Write down exactly which categories are theirs and which are yours before you compare any prices.

## The questions that decide it

1. **How many hours can you give this?** Dry hire is a project. If you can find those hours, or pay a coordinator to, it opens up spaces a package venue cannot offer.
2. **How much do you care about the food?** This is the single most common reason to accept the extra work of dry hire.
3. **Do you already have suppliers?** If you do, restrictions cost you real money and goodwill. If you do not, a venue's list is a genuine service.
4. **What is your tolerance for surprises?** Packages trade flexibility for predictability. That is a fair trade if predictability is what you want.
5. **How far away is the venue?** Coordinating a dozen suppliers you cannot easily visit is harder than it sounds.

## Pricing them honestly

Dry hire looks cheap because the hire fee is the only number on the page. Before you compare it to a package, build the rest of it out:

- Catering, including staff and their meals
- Bar — stock, licence, staff, and the corkage the venue may still charge
- Furniture, linen, crockery, glassware, and their delivery and collection
- Lighting, power and any generator
- Setup, clearing and cleaning
- A coordinator, if you are not doing it yourself
- Contingency, because in a dry hire every gap is yours to fill

If the built-out dry hire figure is close to the package figure, take the package unless you specifically want the control. If it is meaningfully lower and you have the time, dry hire will get you a more distinctive day for the money.

## One thing that is true of both

Whichever you pick, the contract is the product. Read the cancellation ladder, the final-numbers deadline, and any clause allowing prices to rise before the date. Those three paragraphs will affect you more than the format ever will.
